\title{\Large\bfseries Package Hallucinations Revisited: Measurement Validity, Replication, and Tail Risk in Modern Code Models}
\author{Jacob Anderson\\ \small\texttt{jwa@beyond-ordinary.com}}
\date{\small Manuscript draft, September 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Package hallucinations---recommendations of package names that do not exist on the target
registry---create correctness and software-supply-chain risk, but published rates can conflate
model behavior with measurement artifacts. We reproduce the historical model comparison of
Spracklen et al.\ under its own protocol with three generation seeds per model, validate a
grammar-aware parser that treats non-conforming responses as abstentions, run a deterministic
paired token-cap diagnostic, and benchmark six current hosted and local models on 800 fixed Python
prompts with two package queries each, under a preregistered two-track design; separately frozen
extension cells add Qwen 3.5, Kimi K2.7, and Kimi K3. CodeLlama reproduces near the published value
(seed mean 24.55\% versus 26.12\%), and DeepSeek preserves the historical ordering and broad
magnitude, but its family-parser estimates of 13.28--13.54\% fall below the published 16.61\% with
every seed interval excluding it. The large token-cap association reported in our earlier
replication and its independent audit \citep{anderson2026replication,codex2026audit} does not
reproduce as a systematic or monotone effect. Primary unregistered-PyPI recommendation rates for modern models
range from 1.31\% to 17.91\%, yet concentration analysis reveals three qualitatively different
regimes: three responses supply 82.88\% of Grok~4.6's and 72.42\% of DeepSeek V4 Flash's
unregistered recommendations, whereas DeepSeek Coder V2's 10.30\% is diffuse. The Kimi K3 cell
extends the range to 33.30\%, where 46 capped responses supply 81.02\% of unregistered occurrences
despite lower prompt-level risk than its predecessor---an aggregation reversal. Rescoring identical
outputs with the code-import extractor of a concurrent 2026 replication leaves the original six cells
at 8.30--17.49\%, and prompt-conditioned overlap shows that four of the five names shared by all six
models arise on an identical prompt. Valid evaluation requires registry-versioned scoring, explicit
parser coverage, prompt-cluster uncertainty, and concentration diagnostics; a single average rate is
inadequate.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Motivation}

Code-generating language models do not only write code; they recommend dependencies. When a
developer asks which packages a task needs, the model answers with names, and when one of those
names is not registered on the package index, anyone can register it. This is the
\emph{slopsquatting} threat \citep{nesbitt2025slopsquatting,gooding2025slopsquatting}: an adversary
registers a malicious package under a name that models are already priming developers to install
\citep{lanyado2023trust,lanyado2024diving,spracklen2025package}. Unlike typosquatting
\citep{neupane2023beyond,ohm2020backstabber,zimmermann2019small,ladisa2023sok}, the attacker
neither waits for a typo nor needs a name leak; the model supplies the name and the confidence.

Two distinctions matter before anything is counted. First, a recommendation and a generated import
are different observations: an install directive or an answer to ``which packages do I need'' is
an explicit recommendation, whereas an import inside generated code may denote a project-local
module. Second, an unregistered name is not necessarily a fabricated one. Models routinely name
real system, \texttt{apt}, npm, Java, or Go artifacts that are simply not installable from PyPI, and
they routinely name standard-library modules as if they were packages. Both are unsafe PyPI
recommendations; they are different semantic failures. We therefore measure an
\emph{unregistered-PyPI recommendation rate} and reserve the word ``hallucination'' for the
historical metric we replicate.

The replication problem follows directly. A reported rate depends on how free-form model text is
converted into package tokens, on which registry snapshot is treated as ground truth, on the token
budget the model was allowed, and on how the numerator is distributed across responses. None of
these is a clerical detail. Each one changed a scientific conclusion in the work reported here.

\subsection{Gap}

Spracklen et al.\ \citep{spracklen2025package} established the phenomenon at scale: across 16
models and 576,000 code samples, 19.7\% of recommended packages were hallucinated, and 205,474
unique hallucinated names were observed. A concurrent 2026 replication on five frontier models
\citep{churilov2026range} reports a compressed inter-model range and a model-agnostic set of
universally hallucinated names. Both report average package-level rates. Our own earlier attempts
to replicate the 2024 result through a provider-agnostic HTTP client \citep{anderson2026replication},
audited independently \citep{codex2026audit}, exposed four ways an average can mislead: a permissive comma-splitting
parser that scores code fragments and prose as hallucinated packages, truncation that either
silently zeroes a reasoning model or cuts a list mid-name, registry drift that reclassifies names
without any change in the model, and model-artifact ambiguity between a 2024 checkpoint and its
2026 serving stack. Averages also do not reveal whether risk is routine and diffuse or concentrated
in rare recommendation floods, which call for different mitigations.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item A powered, seed-repeated artifact replication that separates broad reproduction from exact
  numerical replication of the 2024 result (\S\ref{sec:tracka}).
\item A validated grammar-aware parser with explicit abstention and response-format outcomes,
  audited on hosted-model formats after the campaign (\S\ref{sec:instrument}, \S\ref{sec:validation}).
\item A deterministic paired token-cap diagnostic that tests a previously suspected mechanism
  (\S\ref{sec:cap}).
\item A six-model modern benchmark with dual registries, prompt-cluster inference, and a
  diffuse-versus-catastrophic tail characterization (\S\ref{sec:trackb}--\S\ref{sec:pairwise}),
  extended by three separately frozen cloud cells whose successor comparison exposes an
  aggregation reversal (\S\ref{sec:extension}, \S\ref{sec:k3}).
\item A direct bridge to the concurrent Churilov study that holds model outputs fixed while
  changing the extractor, plus a prompt-conditioned overlap analysis that distinguishes shared
  elicitation from model-independent names (\S\ref{sec:bridge}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Result preview}

CodeLlama 7B measures 24.55\% (seed mean) under the generic parser against a published 26.12\%;
DeepSeek Coder 6.7B measures 13.43\% under its historical family parser against 16.61\%, with all
three seed intervals excluding the published value. The paired cap diagnostic finds no systematic or
monotone association. Modern primary rates span 1.31--17.91\% with radically different concentration.
The code-import bridge leaves the original Campaign~4 cells at 8.30--17.49\% and adds extension
cells at 6.51\% (Qwen 3.5), 7.38\% (Kimi K2.7), and 8.37\% (Kimi K3); 608 of 621 flagged mentions
(97.91\%) arise from imports rather than explicit install directives. Five unregistered names span all
six Campaign~4 models, but four of them have same-prompt support across all six. The cloud extension
adds a low-rate Qwen 3.5 cell (2.77\%; 95\% cluster interval 2.07--3.54) and a Kimi K2.7 list-flood
failure whose 23.10\% aggregate falls to 4.95\% among responses with at most ten parsed packages
and rises steeply with package position. Kimi K3 scores 33.30\% (27.22--38.81) occurrence-weighted
with 21.50\% prompt risk and a 4.18\% response-macro mean; after diagnostic exclusion of its 46 exact
package cap hits, K3 and K2.7 are 13.11\% and 13.18\%.

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Package hallucinations and supply-chain risk}

The original study \citep{spracklen2025package} measures package hallucination through three
channels: \texttt{pip install} and \texttt{npm install} directives parsed out of generated code, a
query asking which packages the model's own generated code requires, and a query asking which
packages would be useful for the original task. Extracted names are normalized and compared against
a master list of registry names snapshotted on 2024-01-10; any absent name is a hallucination. The
threat model is an unprivileged adversary who can register packages, and the study's responsible
disclosure withheld the hallucinated names themselves. We adopt the same threat model and the same
release policy: no candidate unregistered name appears in this paper or its artifacts. The original
study describes the resulting threat as a variation of the classical package confusion attack; the
name \emph{slopsquatting}, modeled on typosquatting, was coined by Seth Larson and popularized by
Andrew Nesbitt in April 2025 \citep{nesbitt2025slopsquatting,gooding2025slopsquatting}, roughly ten
months after the study's preprint appeared.

Three terms recur below. A \emph{package recommendation} is a name the model offered in answer to a
package query; a \emph{generated import} is a name that appears in an import statement of generated
code. An \emph{unregistered name} is absent from the relevant PyPI snapshot. An \emph{ecosystem
mismatch} is an unregistered name that denotes a real artifact elsewhere. All three are relevant to
slopsquatting; only the first two are directly comparable across studies that use the same channel.

\subsection{Evaluation of code-generating models}

Benchmarks of code models \citep{chen2021codex} pin model versions and report a single number per
model; hosted models make pinning harder, because an alias can change what it serves. Stochastic
decoding means a single run is a draw, and comparisons across models on the same prompts should be
paired by prompt. Most importantly for this measurement, per-package observations are not
independent: the several names in one response share a prompt, a task, and a generation trajectory.
Treating them as independent Bernoulli trials is pseudoreplication \citep{hurlbert1984pseudoreplication};
the appropriate unit of resampling is the cluster \citep{cameron2008bootstrap,efron1993bootstrap}.

\subsection{Measurement error in free-form generations}

Converting free-form text into package tokens is a classification task with its own precision and
recall. A permissive parser has high recall and manufactures false positives when the model drifts
out of the requested format; a strict parser abstains on non-conforming responses and loses genuine
recommendations embedded in prose. Either choice is a measurement decision that must be reported
with the rate. Registry membership is also time-dependent: names are registered and deleted after
any snapshot, so a name can move from ``hallucinated'' to registered, or from registered to
deleted, with no change in the model. Finally, the response format itself is a variable. A token cap
truncates lists; a reasoning model may spend its whole budget on hidden reasoning and return nothing;
a hosted endpoint may leak a literal control token into content. Each of these can be mistaken for
model behavior.

\subsection{Concurrent 2026 replication}

Churilov \citep{churilov2026range} re-ran the original prompt corpus in full (199,845 Python and
JavaScript generations) on five frontier models released between October 2025 and March 2026,
extracting package references from generated code (install directives plus imports) and validating
against the original master lists. Beyond replication, that work identifies 127 names hallucinated by
all five models, of which 53 remained registrable after coordinated disclosure with PyPI Security and
Socket.dev, documents a Python-over-JavaScript asymmetry, and reports a Haiku-below-Sonnet inversion.
It is the broader study in languages, corpus size, registrability testing, and disclosure, and it
establishes that the cross-model attack surface is operationally real.

Three points of interpretation matter for the comparison in \S\ref{sec:bridge}. First, its
code-import estimand differs from the original study's three-channel measurement and from our
package-recommendation estimand; a rate from one is not directly a rate from the other. Second, its
reported range compression (4.62--6.10\% overall, 5.49--7.27\% on Python, against 5.2\% and 21.7\%)
compares a five-model frontier cohort with the original study's commercial and open-source
\emph{category averages}, not with the original model-level extrema, which spanned 3.59\% to 26.12\%
on Python. Third, its Wilson intervals, chi-square tests, and permutation tests treat each extracted
package mention as an independent observation, although mentions cluster within generations; we do
not assert that its significant pairs would vanish under cluster-aware inference, only that they were
not computed under it. Its interpretation of Jaccard overlap as evidence of shared training origins
is one hypothesis; shared prompt elicitation, extractor behavior, and corpus composition are
alternatives that \S\ref{sec:overlap} tests directly.

\subsection{Research questions}

\begin{description}[style=unboxed,leftmargin=0pt,itemsep=2pt]
\item[RQ1 (Replication).] Do the published CodeLlama and DeepSeek results reproduce with a larger
  fixed prompt set and repeated generation seeds?
\item[RQ2 (Measurement validity).] How much do parser family, strict grammar, response format, and
  registry date alter reported rates?
\item[RQ3 (Cap sensitivity).] Does package hallucination vary systematically with the package-response
  token cap when code and prompts are paired and generation is deterministic?
\item[RQ4 (Modern models).] How do current hosted and local models compare under a common prompt set,
  frozen parser, and dual-registry definition?
\item[RQ5 (Failure shape).] Are aggregate rates driven by diffuse errors or by rare catastrophic
  recommendation floods?
\item[RQ6 (Concurrent-work bridge).] When identical outputs are rescored with Churilov's code-import
  extractor, and cross-model overlap is conditioned on prompt identity, which apparent agreements and
  disagreements remain?
\item[RQ7 (Successor and list-volume mechanism).] Do the Kimi K2.7 response-volume and late-position
  effects persist in Kimi K3 when finish reason and token use are retained for every response?
\end{description}

\subsection{Two-track design}

Figure~\ref{fig:design} summarizes the design, which was frozen as a preregistration before data
collection with every deviation logged as a dated amendment \citep{nosek2018preregistration}.
\textbf{Track A} is historical artifact replication: the original model families, the paper's
sampling settings and caps, the paper's family parsers, and the frozen 2024 registry. It licenses
statements about whether the 2024 result reproduces. \textbf{Track B} is a modern benchmark: the
same prompts, a validated grammar-aware parser (Parser~v2), larger token budgets, and both the 2024
and 2026 registries. It licenses cross-model statements under one instrument and is explicitly not a
scale reproduction of the 2024 study. \textbf{Extension cells} were added later under separately
frozen protocols: Qwen 3.5 Cloud and Kimi K2.7 Code Cloud under a preregistration frozen on
2026-08-14 (with a logged substitution of K2.7 for K3 when the K3 endpoint returned HTTP~402 before
any generation), and Kimi K3 under a later successor/mechanism specification. These cells extend but
do not alter Track B's original inferential family.

For Kimi K3 the word ``frozen'' is used narrowly. Prompts, parser, registries, model and sampling
settings, estimands, diagnostics, and the comparison family were fixed before outcome inspection.
Provenance and completion-gate code was expanded while collection was in flight and before any
package response was scored; the live collector had already loaded the earlier implementation. Those
edits did not change prompts, responses, parser decisions, or outcome calculations, but the
executable pipeline was not byte-frozen end to end, and because the K3 preregistration was not
committed before collection the run is retrospectively rather than cryptographically preregistered.
A retrospective provenance stamp hashes the attested design core, the local chronology, every
commit-candidate file, and a content-addressed index of the 93 raw-artifact files; it states plainly
that it does not repair the missing pre-data commit. The repository now enforces a commit-based
freeze gate for any future analytic cell.

\subsection{Prompt corpus and unit of analysis}

The original artifact provides four Python prompt datasets: Stack Overflow questions (all-time and
last-year popularity) and LLM-generated prompts based on popular packages on the same two time bases.
Because the Stack Overflow datasets are ordered by popularity, a prefix is a biased subset; we draw
200 prompts per dataset with a fixed seed and use that one subset for every cell, seed, and phase.
Each prompt yields one code generation and two package responses, so a complete cell contains 800
prompts and 1,600 package responses. The prompt is the clustering and pairing unit for every interval
and comparison.

\subsection{Parser v2 and response statuses}
\label{sec:instrument}

Parser~v2 accepts a response only if it conforms to a comma or line list grammar whose items are
PEP~503-style name tokens \citep{pep503}. List markers such as ``12.'' or ``-'' are stripped only
when anchored at the start of an item, fixing a legacy normalizer that turned ``12. requests'' into
``1requests''; wrapper characters are stripped only in matched pairs; names are deduplicated within
a response. Every response receives one of three statuses: \emph{list}, \emph{malformed} (code
fences, prose, or any non-name item), or \emph{empty}. Malformed responses are abstentions: they
contribute no package to the denominator and are reported as an outcome. This is the deliberate
trade of recall for precision that \S\ref{sec:validation} quantifies.

\subsection{Registries and categories}

Two PyPI snapshots are used: the frozen 2024-01-10 master list from the original artifact (500,513
names) and a snapshot fetched at campaign start on 2026-08-12 (869,894 names; the namespace grew by
74\%). The primary metric counts a recommended name only if it is absent from \emph{both} snapshots
and is not a standard-library module. Each qualifier earns its place: 8,961 names in the frozen list
have since been deleted from PyPI, including squat-registrations of standard-library names such as
\texttt{os} and \texttt{json}, which means the original methodology silently scored stdlib confusion
as valid packages, and its Python pipeline, unlike its JavaScript one, had no core-module exclusion.
Secondary categories are valid in both snapshots, post-snapshot package, deleted since snapshot,
standard-library confusion, and unregistered in both.

\subsection{Outcomes}

Every cell co-reports: the unregistered-package occurrence rate (unregistered occurrences over
accepted package occurrences); the prompt-level probability of at least one unregistered
recommendation (\emph{prompt risk}); malformed and empty response rates; packages per prompt and per
accepted response; unique unregistered names; top-one, top-three, and top-ten response shares with
leave-top-$k$ diagnostics; and, for the cap diagnostic and extension cells, position-band rates.
Leave-top-$k$ estimates characterize concentration; they are not alternative primary estimands.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

All intervals are dataset-stratified prompt-cluster percentile bootstraps: prompts are resampled with
replacement within each of the four 200-prompt datasets, and the ratio of interest is recomputed in
each replicate (50,000 replicates for cell intervals and pairwise comparisons; 20,000 for the cap
diagnostic). Cross-cell comparisons are paired by prompt on the 800 shared prompts, with Holm
correction \citep{holm1979simple} across the 15 Track B pairs. The preregistration fixed the
bootstrap but not the exact $p$-value convention; the campaign therefore reports the preregistered
two-sided bootstrap-tail probability and, as a labeled post-hoc sensitivity, a recentered-null
bootstrap with the same replicates, strata, pairing, and seed. Effect sizes and intervals are
primary. Adjusted values at the finite-bootstrap resolution floor are reported as bounds: for the
eight-comparison K3 family, $8 \times 1/50{,}000 = 0.000160$ is written $p \le 0.000160$, never as an
exact value. The post-hoc K2.7/K3 prompt-risk and flood-frequency comparisons use the same
dataset-stratified paired resampling.

\subsection{Churilov bridge and prompt-conditioned overlap}

To compare with the concurrent study without regenerating anything, the frozen generated code of
every cell was rescored with a port of the public Churilov extraction design: a \texttt{pip install}
regex plus top-level imports (AST first, tree-sitter fallback), the artifact's stdlib list and
false-positive filter, imports deduplicated within a generation, and no cross-channel deduplication
against install hits. Both the frozen-registry absence rate (the closest artifact-level bridge) and
our dual-registry, non-stdlib rate are reported with prompt-cluster intervals stratified by dataset.
For overlap, ordinary Jaccard over unique unregistered names is reported beside an aligned Jaccard
over (normalized name, dataset, prompt index) tuples, and for every pairwise and all-model
intersection we count how many shared names occur on at least one identical prompt. No candidate
name is published.

\subsection{Cloud extension and per-response provenance}

The Qwen 3.5 and Kimi K2.7 cells reuse the fixed subset, Parser~v2, dual registries, and the
two-query design, with a separate 13-comparison Holm family for comparisons involving a new cell.
\qone\ and \qtwo\ volumes are co-reported because the two prompts are measurement channels, not
interchangeable replicates, once a model enumerates hundreds of names in one channel. Kimi K2.7's
combined preregistered estimate is preserved; its query-, volume-, and position-specific diagnostics
are post hoc. The first extension runner retained cap hits only at phase level, so K2.7 cap membership
is a frozen within-phase longest-response \emph{proxy}. Kimi K3 corrected that instrumentation:
every response carries an ordered sidecar with finish reason, served model, prompt and completion
tokens, and exact cap status, and scoring fails if any of the 2,400 rows is missing. Provider-reported
token cost was recorded against a maintainer-authorized run-level dollar guard.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Measurement validation}
\label{sec:validation}

The instrument changed the scientific conclusion, so we report it first (Table~\ref{tab:parser}).
On the initial stratified 200-response validation sample, Parser~v2 classified response status with
98\% accuracy; all four errors share one root cause (wrapper characters around an entire comma
list), and no list-classified record contained an incorrectly extracted token. Grammar-valid
occurrence recall is 97.78\%, but end-to-end semantic recall is 79.75\%: 102 recommendation
occurrences sat inside prose or code that the grammar refuses to mine. That is the deliberate cost of
strict abstention, and it is why malformed rates accompany every rate below. The validation sample
predates the hosted cells, so a post-campaign audit drew 180 Track B responses (20 uniformly random
and 10 deliberately difficult format or tail cases per model). All 180 agreed with the frozen contract
on both status and exact extraction.

The audit also surfaced two sensitivities that contract agreement cannot see. Grok 4.6 appended a
literal \texttt{<|eos|>} marker to 184 responses; all 184 are malformed under the frozen grammar and
become valid lists when, and only when, that exact terminal marker is removed. Stripping it changes
Grok's malformed rate from 12.56\% to 1.06\% but its primary rate only from 8.22\% to 7.91\%, because
the 262 recovered names are registered or standard-library names. DeepSeek Coder V2 produced the
collapsed token \texttt{NoPythonpackagesrequired} eleven times, which the grammar accepts as a name;
treating such one-token answers as no-package responses moves its rate from 10.30\% to 9.89\% (and
gpt-oss from 2.15\% to 2.14\%). The frozen results remain primary; both adjustments are reported as
transport and semantic sensitivities.

Registry time changes the label space (Table~\ref{tab:categories}). Standard-library mentions are
10--20\% of accepted occurrences in every cell, peaking at DeepSeek Coder V2, and the original
methodology could not see them. Scoring against the current snapshot alone inflates every rate
because the purged stdlib squats now count as absent; scoring against the frozen snapshot alone
counts post-snapshot registrations as hallucinations and squatted stdlib names as valid. Only the
dual-registry, non-stdlib definition is stable in both directions.

\subsection{Historical replication}
\label{sec:tracka}

Figure~\ref{fig:tracka} and Table~\ref{tab:tracka} give Track A. Under the paper's exact protocol

\subsection{Controlled cap diagnostic}
\label{sec:cap}

Earlier versions of this replication \citep{anderson2026replication} attributed an 11.5\pp\ increase
in DeepSeek's measured rate at a raised cap to truncation suppressing genuinely hallucinated tails.
The independent audit \citep{codex2026audit} identified
the dominant mechanism as evaluator contamination: at the larger budget DeepSeek drifted out of the
comma-list grammar (code fences in 338 of 800 long responses versus 132 of 800 short), and the
permissive parser scored code fragments and prose as hallucinated packages. That decomposition
motivated a controlled test. Both DeepSeek generations were queried over the same code and prompts at
temperature 0 with a fixed seed, one worker, and counterbalanced caps of 64, 128, 256, 512, and 2048
tokens, with prefix-verified pairing and Parser~v2 (Figure~\ref{fig:cap};
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:capcells}).

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_cap_diagnostic}
\caption{Controlled cap diagnostic. (A) Unregistered rate by package-response cap. (B) Malformed share
by cap. (C) Each cap paired with the 2048-token response at the prompt level, with 95\%
dataset-stratified paired bootstrap intervals. Every interval includes zero; no equivalence margin was
preregistered.}
\label{fig:cap}
\end{figure*}

DeepSeek Coder V2 16B measures 7.10--7.48\% across the five caps; every paired delta versus 2048 is at
most 0.33\pp, and all intervals include zero. DeepSeek Coder 6.7B is hump-shaped rather than
monotone: 5.28\%, 5.81\%, 6.72\%, 5.12\%, and 4.78\%. All paired intervals include zero, although the
cap-256 interval is wide ($-0.04$ to $4.29$\pp). Malformed rates are stable across caps (about 12\%
and 53\% respectively). The supported statement is that the large historical cap association did
not reproduce as a systematic or monotone effect in the deterministic paired diagnostic. It is not a
proof of equivalence or of a zero causal effect: no equivalence margin was preregistered, some paired
generations still diverged before the truncation point, and temperature was not crossed factorially
with cap. What remains true from the earlier work is that a 64-token cap silently zeroes a reasoning
model (gpt-oss 20B returned empty content on 15 of 15 probe queries, versus 0 of 15 for a CodeLlama
control), and that cap-truncated list tails interact with any parser.

\subsection{Failure regimes and tail risk}
\label{sec:tail}

The signature result is that similar-looking averages conceal operationally different risks
(Figures~\ref{fig:concentration} and \ref{fig:leavetopk}). Ranking prompts by their unregistered
count and accumulating their share separates three regimes.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_concentration}
\caption{Ranked-prompt cumulative concentration curves. Each curve is the share of a cell's
unregistered occurrences supplied by its $k$ worst prompts. (A) Campaign~4 cells. (B) Extension
cells. Grok 4.6 and DeepSeek V4 Flash reach half their total within one and two prompts; the
low-rate cells and DeepSeek Coder V2 need 29--52 prompts.}
\label{fig:concentration}
\end{figure*}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_leave_topk}
\caption{Leave-top-$k$ sensitivity: the rate after removing the $k$ worst responses, for $k$ of 0,
1, 3, and 10. Flat lines are diffuse failures; steep lines are catastrophic tails. Leave-top-$k$ is a
concentration diagnostic, not an alternative estimand.}
\label{fig:leavetopk}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Low-rate, diffuse failures.} For Opus, GPT-5.2, and gpt-oss, the three worst responses
supply 5--11\% of unregistered occurrences, and removing them moves the rate by only 0.10--0.13\pp\
(1.31 to 1.18, 1.88 to 1.74, 2.15 to 2.05). Half of each cell's total requires 29--38 prompts. The
median prompt has zero unregistered names and the maximum is 3--7.

\paragraph{High-rate, diffuse failure.} DeepSeek Coder V2's top three responses contribute only
3.98\% of its unregistered occurrences; removing them leaves 9.93\%, and removing ten leaves 9.43\%.
Its risk is everywhere: 21.0\% of prompts yield at least one unregistered name, the highest prompt
risk in the cohort.

\paragraph{Catastrophic-tail failure.} One Grok 4.6 response supplies 63.06\% of its unregistered
occurrences (350 names in a single answer) and three supply 82.88\%; removing those three reduces
Grok from 8.22\% to 1.61\%, and removing ten leaves 0.80\%. Two DeepSeek V4 Flash prompts supply
half its total, three responses supply 72.42\%, and the leave-three rate is 5.71\%. Both cells have the
\emph{lowest} prompt risk in the cohort (5.5\% and 18.1\%): a typical Grok or V4 Flash answer is fine,
and the tail free-associates entire package inventories. That concentration is also what destroys
their statistical resolution.

The practical consequence is that the same average implies different mitigations. Diffuse failure
calls for routine validation of every recommended name at install time; catastrophic tails call for
flood detection, output bounds, and refusal to enumerate. A leaderboard that ranks Grok 4.6 (8.22\%)
next to DeepSeek Coder V2 (10.30\%) as neighbors hides that they fail in different ways.

\subsection{Kimi K3 successor test}
\label{sec:k3}

The 2,400-call Kimi K3 cell passed all twelve response-sidecar gates: one served id, zero errors or
request adjustments, 72 exact cap hits (46 in package responses, all in \qtwo), and \$16.27 of
token-derived analytic spend (Table~\ref{tab:k3}). Its primary occurrence-weighted rate is 33.30\%
(27.22--38.81) with 21.50\% prompt risk, 4.19\% malformed responses, and 25,889 parsed occurrences.
All eight separately adjusted paired comparisons place K3 above its comparator on this estimand
(Appendix Table~\ref{tab:k3pairs}); K2.7 minus K3 is $-10.20$\pp\ ($-16.43$ to $-3.44$).

The task split is stark: \qone\ is 1.76\% over 1,303 occurrences, \qtwo\ is 34.97\% over 24,586. K3
follows the requested-code query well and sometimes treats the open recommendation query as an
unbounded enumeration task. The exact cap decomposition explains the headline: the 46 package cap
hits are 2.88\% of package responses but supply 13,408 occurrences (51.79\% of the total) and 6,985
unregistered occurrences (81.02\%) at a 52.10\% within-tail rate. The exact-cap-excluded diagnostic is
13.11\%. We keep 33.30\% primary and label exclusion as post-hoc conditioning on a post-treatment
variable \citep{montgomery2018conditioning}, not as a corrected estimate.

The comparison with K2.7 is an aggregation reversal (Table~\ref{tab:k27k3}, Figure~\ref{fig:k3}).
K3 has lower prompt risk (21.50\% versus 43.00\%), a lower response-macro mean (4.18\% versus 8.36\%),
and only 54 \qtwo\ responses above 100 packages versus 234; on the 800 shared prompts the paired
\qtwo\ prompt-risk difference is 20.63\pp\ (16.50--24.75) and the flood-frequency difference is

shared; but the flood-prompt Jaccard of 0.083 against a marginal-constrained maximum of 0.231 shows
that susceptibility does not alone determine which model floods. The 54 K3 floods contain 8,010 unique
normalized unregistered names with mean pairwise Jaccard 0.0011 and no name in at least a quarter of
floods, which rules out a repeated fixed catalog or a degenerate repetition loop as the main
explanation of the tail; it does not remove the parser's ordinary validity limitations.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{The parser is part of the measurement instrument}

Strict parsing bought precision by selectively abstaining, and the price was visible: 79.75\%
end-to-end recall in validation, and malformed shares from 0.44\% (gpt-oss) to 18.25\% (DeepSeek
Coder V2) in the benchmark. Coverage differences of that size can bias cross-model comparisons if
they are omitted, because the denominator of a low-coverage cell is drawn from the subset of its
responses that stayed on format. Transport-layer control tokens should be normalized before parsing
and logged; the Grok \texttt{<|eos|>} case shows that a hosted endpoint can move a malformed rate by
eleven points without moving the primary rate.

\subsection{Registry time changes the label space}

A name can move from ``hallucinated'' to registered, or from registered to deleted, with no change in
the model, and the 2024 registry contained squatted standard-library names that made module confusion
invisible. Registry snapshots should be dated and digested exactly like model artifacts, and rates
should be scored against a frozen and a current snapshot with an explicit stdlib policy. The bridge
shows the corollary: once the extractor is held fixed, registry drift explains almost none of the gap
between studies, so a disagreement between two 2026 rates is about cohorts, prompts, and extraction
semantics, not about the calendar.

\subsection{Average rates conceal operationally different risks}

Three regimes appeared in six modern models: low and diffuse, high and diffuse, and catastrophic
tail. Tail-aware metrics are necessary for a security evaluation because the mitigations differ.
Diffuse risk is addressed by registry checks at install time and by installation confirmation
interfaces; catastrophic tails are addressed by flood guards and output bounds on enumeration. The
K2.7/K3 reversal is the clearest example: K3 is safer on typical-prompt measures and worse when every
emitted occurrence receives equal weight. Neither estimand subsumes the other; a report that carries
only one of them answers only one operational question.

\subsection{What the cap diagnostic resolves, and what it does not}

Under deterministic, contamination-free measurement the proposed cap mechanism for DeepSeek is
weakened: rates are flat or non-monotone across a 32-fold range of caps with every paired interval
including zero. The diagnostic does not identify temperature as the cause of the earlier association
\citep{anderson2026replication} and does not establish formal equivalence. The exact K3 finish reasons then show a different relation
between caps and outcomes: cap-associated responses can dominate an aggregate, but cap membership is
observed after generation and cannot distinguish runaway enumeration from the boundary that truncates
it. A randomized within-prompt cap experiment is still required.

\subsection{Replication as measurement debugging}

The useful verdict here is not binary. Artifact fidelity (does the transport reproduce the artifact),
measurement validity (does the instrument measure package recommendations), and contemporary
relevance (what do current models do) are separate questions with separate evidence. ``Broad
reproduction with exact numerical failure'' is more informative than ``replicated'' or ``failed to
replicate'' because it locates the residual in checkpoint provenance rather than in the phenomenon,
and it is only visible because the parser and cap confounds were removed first.

\subsection{What the concurrent replication changes}

Churilov establishes breadth and a real, registrable cross-model attack surface. Our bridge shows
that matching the extractor is necessary but not sufficient for rate comparability: cohort, prompt
source, the module-to-distribution mapping, refusal policy, and the inferential unit remain
consequential. Prompt-conditioned overlap should become standard before universal-name sets are used
to infer shared training origins, since four of five all-model names in our cohort are explained by
same-prompt elicitation. Two concrete additions that would strengthen that study without new model
runs are the current-registry revalidation it defers and a generation-clustered recomputation of its
pairwise significance.

\section{Threats to validity and limitations}
\label{sec:threats}

\begin{itemize}
\item Python only, and a package-query task rather than general code generation.
\item The six-cell benchmark, the two separately frozen extension cells, and the K3 successor cell
  are purposive, not representative samples of frontier or local models.
\item Kimi K3's scientific design was frozen, but its provenance and completion gates were expanded
  during collection; the finalized manifest's missing later fields corroborate that the live process
  had loaded an earlier pipeline version. Those edits did not affect prompts, responses, parsing, or
  estimands, but ``frozen pipeline'' would overstate the record, and the K3 freeze rests on a
  retrospective attestation and later remote commit, not a tamper-evident pre-data commit.
\item One fixed prompt subset and one modern generation draw per model; between-run variance for
  Track B is bounded only by Track A's seed evidence.
\item Parser~v2 has 79.75\% end-to-end semantic recall in the initial validation sample; the Track B
  audit was AI-reviewed at maintainer direction, and no human labeled every record.
\item Occurrence rates condition on accepted grammar-valid package tokens.
\item Hosted aliases and provider-side infrastructure can change despite served-id capture; the
  DeepSeek Coder V2 resumed-run metadata are mixed.
\item Some package-query responses hit token caps, so catastrophic-tail totals may be lower bounds.
\item Kimi K2.7's phase-level cap counts cannot be mapped to exact response ids; its exclusion
  analysis is a longest-response proxy. K3's exact cap membership is observed but cap-excluded
  conditioning remains post-treatment, and the K2.7/K3 comparison mixes exact labels with a proxy.
\item Kimi K2.7's aggregate denominator is overwhelmingly generated by extreme \qtwo\ lists;
  occurrence-weighted and prompt-level risk answer different operational questions.
\item Unregistered PyPI names include ecosystem mismatches, not only invented strings.
\item All tail, EOS, sentinel, recentered-bootstrap, bridge, and cap-conditioning analyses are post hoc
  and remain labeled as such; the bridge cohorts have no exact model match, and generated imports can
  denote local modules.
\item The Qwen 3.5 and Kimi K2.7 cells are a separately preregistered extension and are not folded
  retroactively into Campaign~4's confirmatory family.
\end{itemize}

We also list claims the evidence does not support. Grok 4.6 and DeepSeek V4 Flash refute the
generalization that frontier models hallucinate at 1.3--2.2\%, and gpt-oss is not a hosted frontier
model. The cap diagnostic supports non-reproduction, not causal elimination. Not every unregistered
name is fabricated. Only five model pairs are definitively different. Parser~v2's grammar is
reliable, but strict abstention, transport markers, and collapsed no-package tokens are measurable
limitations. Churilov's results and ours do not agree once extraction is matched. Universal names do
not prove shared training data. Kimi K2.7 does not have a model-wide 23.10\% ordinary recommendation
rate, and removing cap-hit responses does not ``repair'' it. Kimi K3 is not simply worse than K2.7;
the ranking depends on the operational question. The token cap is not established as the cause of
K3's tail. And the low flood-prompt Jaccard does not make prompt susceptibility irrelevant.

\section{Security, ethics, and responsible artifact release}
\label{sec:ethics}

Publishing currently unregistered candidate package names would hand an adversary a target list.
Following the original study, this paper and every committed artifact release aggregate and
per-prompt counts, hashes, manifests, model ids and digests, parser version, and registry snapshot
identities, but never the names. Raw responses are retained outside version control under a
content-addressed index and can be shared with verified researchers under a responsible-access
policy. The work measures unsafe recommendations; it does not validate that any named package is
malicious, and a name registered after a model first recommended it is indistinguishable here from
an unrelated coincidence.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The historical pattern broadly reproduces, but the exact DeepSeek value does not under its own family
parser. Modern models show distinct diffuse and catastrophic-tail failure regimes that a single
average conceals, and a successor model can be safer on typical prompts while scoring worse
occurrence-weighted. Future package-hallucination evaluations should version the registry and the
parser, report response coverage beside every rate, cluster inference by prompt, and quantify
concentration. The next experiment implied by these results is a within-prompt package-cap by
bounded-list factorial, launched through a commit-based freeze gate, to separate runaway enumeration
from token-boundary truncation and to test a practical mitigation.

\label{app:artifacts}

\begin{sloppypar}
The repository accompanying this paper contains: the full preregistrations and their chronological
amendments (\texttt{Experiments/PREREGISTRATION\_v4.md},
\texttt{PREREGISTRATION\_OLLAMA\_CLOUD\_EXTENSION.md}, \texttt{PREREGISTRATION\_KIMI\_K3\_EXTENSION.md});
the frozen Parser~v2 grammar and unit tests (\texttt{parser\_v2.py}); the initial and Track B audit
designs, signed summaries, and hashes; dual-registry construction, dates, and counts; all seed-level
Track A tables and the full cap pairing and position-band tables (\texttt{campaign4\_results.json},
\texttt{cap\_diagnostic\_summary.json}); all 15 paired comparisons under both conventions
(\texttt{campaign4\_post\_analysis.json}); prompt-level derived counts sufficient to reproduce every
interval without exposing raw names; the DeepSeek Coder V2 run-history addendum; the Churilov-compatible
extractor port and its machine-readable output (\texttt{churilov\_bridge\_analysis.py}, \texttt{.json});
the separately frozen extension protocol, manifests, and post-analysis
(\texttt{ollama\_cloud\_extension\_results.json}, \texttt{ollama\_cloud\_extension\_post\_analysis.json});
the Kimi K3 ordered response-metadata sidecars, cost ledger, scoring gate, and results
(\texttt{kimi\_k3\_extension\_results.json}); the K2.7/K3 paired post-analysis
(\texttt{kimi\_k2\_7\_vs\_k3\_post\_analysis.json}); the retrospective provenance stamp with its explicit
non-cryptographic-preregistration limitation and raw-artifact hash index; the review amendment ledger;
and the commit-based freeze gate (\texttt{experiment\_gate.py}) required for every future analytic
cell. The manuscript's tables and figures are regenerated from these files by
\texttt{paper/build\_tables.py} and \texttt{paper/make\_figures.py}.
\end{sloppypar}
